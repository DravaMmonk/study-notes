# COMP90049 Lecture 22 - Anomaly Detection

Sources: `lecture/22-anomalydetection.pdf`; Apple Notes: CS/25S2/IML — "W11L22 Anomaly Detection".

This lecture asks: **how do we find data points that look "as if generated by a different mechanism"? Depending on whether labels exist and what shape the data has, which method should we use?**

Learning path: [What an anomaly is](#topic-1) → [Three types of anomaly](#topic-2) → [Three detection paradigms](#topic-3) → [Statistical methods](#topic-4) → [Proximity, density and clustering methods](#topic-5).

Key points: z-score, Mahalanobis distance, kNN-based density and relative density (LOF). Hard parts: why local density can cope with data of uneven density. Common mistakes: an anomaly is not noise; supervised anomaly detection cannot be judged by accuracy.

<a id="topic-1"></a>
## 1. What an anomaly is

### A warning: the ozone hole treated as noise

![Slide 04 - Ozone depletion history](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-04.png)

In 1985, data from the British Antarctic Survey showed Antarctic ozone 10% below normal. NASA's Nimbus 7 satellite carried instruments for measuring ozone too, yet it had not reported such low concentrations — the values were so low that a computer program had treated them as noise and discarded them. The curve on the right is the average area of the Antarctic ozone hole, growing rapidly from the 1980s: the discarded "outliers" were exactly the most important signal.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.4 -->

The applications are broad: banking (unusually large purchases, withdrawals from unusual locations), insurance fraud, bots or hacked accounts on social media, unusual behaviour in surveillance, weather and seismic anomalies, sudden changes in customer behaviour, skin-cancer screening, and, in machine learning, removing outliers from a dataset.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.3 -->

### Definition

![Slide 05 - Anomalies vs noise](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-05.png)

An **anomaly (outlier)** is a data object that deviates significantly from the normal objects, as if generated by **a different mechanism** — for example an unusual credit-card purchase.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.5 -->

**Common mistake: anomaly ≠ noise.** **Noise** is random error or variance in a measured variable and should be removed **before** anomaly detection; an anomaly violates the mechanism that generates the normal data and often corresponds to an important (even critical) real-world problem, such as a network intrusion or credit-card fraud.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.5 -->

### Three forms of the problem

| Form | What to do | When it applies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Threshold detection | Find the points whose anomaly score exceeds a threshold $t$ | You have a scoring function and a threshold learned from labelled normal data |
| Top-n detection | Find the $n$ points with the largest anomaly scores $f(x)$ | Exploratory analysis, flagging only a limited number of the most suspicious points |
| Per-point scoring | Database $D$ holds mostly normal (but unlabelled) points; compute the anomaly score of a new test point $x$ relative to $D$ | New data keeps arriving and must be evaluated one by one |
<!-- Sources: L22 p.6 -->

## 2. Three types of anomaly

### Global (point) anomalies

![Slide 08 - Global anomalies](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-08.png)

A **global (point) anomaly** is a single object that deviates significantly from the rest of the dataset, e.g. an intrusion in a computer network. In the figure $o_1$ lies far from the two groups of normal data $N_1$ and $N_2$ , so it is a global anomaly; the question marks beside $o_2$ and $o_3$ show the difficulty: finding a suitable measure of "deviation".
<!-- Sources: L22 p.8 -->

### Contextual (conditional) anomalies

A **contextual (conditional) anomaly** is anomalous only in a particular context. Attributes split into two groups: **contextual attributes** define the context, such as time and location; **behavioural attributes** describe the object itself and are used to judge whether it is anomalous, such as temperature. It can be seen as a generalisation of local anomalies — the context need not be spatial. The difficulty is defining a meaningful context.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.9 -->

![Slide 10 - Contextual anomaly example](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-10.png)

On the curve of Paris's monthly mean temperature, 10°C at the left circle $t_1$ in winter is normal, while the same 10°C at the right circle $t_2$ in summer is an anomaly — the same value in a different context (month). The slide then asks: what if it were Melbourne? There the weather changes within a day, so this might just be a more complex pattern.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.10-11 -->

### Collective anomalies

![Slide 13 - Collective anomaly example](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-13.png)

A **collective anomaly** is a group of objects that together deviate significantly from the whole, even though each object on its own is not anomalous. In the ECG signal, the dashed circle surrounds a flat subsequence: every point's value is normal, but such a long stretch without any beat is not. Detection must look at relationships between objects, so it applies to sequential (time-series), spatial and graph data. For example, a run of login attempts from different IP addresses in a short time is not suspicious one by one, but together may signal an intrusion.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.12-13 -->

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Global / point anomaly | A single point deviates significantly from the rest of the dataset |
| Contextual / conditional anomaly | Anomalous only in a specific context (time, location, …) |
| Collective anomaly | A group of points is anomalous together, though individual points are not |
<!-- Sources: L22 p.14 -->

<a id="topic-3"></a>
## 3. Three detection paradigms

### Supervised

![Slide 16 - Supervised anomaly detection](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-16.png)

Both normal data and anomalies are labelled (checked by domain experts), so the task becomes binary classification. The challenges the slide lists: labels are needed for both classes; the classes are extremely imbalanced (the anomaly class can be boosted or artificial anomalies made up); unknown, emerging anomalies cannot be detected; accuracy is misleading.

**Common mistake:** in supervised anomaly detection we try to catch as many anomalies as possible, i.e. raise the **recall**; but chasing recall alone produces many false alarms (normal cases flagged as anomalies). So precision must be watched as well (see [08](08-evaluation.en.md)).
<!-- Sources: L22 p.16; Apple Notes W11L22 Anomaly Detection -->

### Semi-supervised and unsupervised

| Paradigm | Labels available | How | Challenges |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Semi-supervised | Only normal data is labelled | Train only on normal data so the model learns what "normal" looks like; apply it to all data and flag whatever falls outside the learned decision boundary (high residual, low probability) as a potential anomaly | Needs labels for the normal class; the false-alarm rate can be high — unseen but legitimate records may be flagged |
| Unsupervised | No labels | Assume normal objects cluster into groups and anomalies lie far from every group; first build a profile of "normal" (summary statistics, a multivariate distribution model), then find points that differ markedly from it | Normal objects may share no strong pattern, while collective anomalies can be very similar within a small region; the false-positive rate can be high while real anomalies are still missed |
<!-- Sources: L22 p.16-19 -->

![Slide 19 - Unsupervised challenges](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-19.png)

Back to the point plot from Section 2: without labels, the method can only rely on the assumption that "normal data forms groups". $o_1$ is easy to spot; but $o_3$ is a few points close together, similar to one another, and may be taken for a small normal group — this is what "collective anomalies can be very similar within a small region" means.

Unsupervised methods fall into four families, covered in the next two sections:

| Method | Assumption |
| --- | --- |
| Statistical (model-based) | Normal data follows some statistical model |
| Proximity-based | An anomaly's nearest neighbours are far away |
| Density-based | Anomalies lie in low-density regions |
| Clustering-based | Normal data belongs to large, dense clusters |
<!-- Sources: L22 p.21 -->

## 4. Statistical methods

![Slide 22 - Statistical anomaly detection](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-22.png)

Idea: fit a parametric distribution $f(x,\theta)$ to the data; objects in the model's low-probability regions are anomalies — the smaller the probability density, the more likely an outlier. The difficulty is that the result depends heavily on whether the statistical model's assumptions hold.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.22 -->

![Slide 23 - Visualising the data](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-23.png)

You can start by plotting: a boxplot in 1-D, a scatter plot in 2-D, a spin plot in 3-D. In the boxplot on the left, the two red circles are points beyond the whiskers; in the scatter plot on the right, the circled points belong to neither of the two groups. The drawback is that this is time-consuming and subjective.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.23 -->

### Univariate: z-score

![Slide 24 - Univariate z-score](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-24.png)

Assume the data is normally distributed and estimate by maximum likelihood

$$
\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i,\qquad
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\mu)^2}.
$$

Choose a confidence limit: the normal curve on the right marks that $\mu\pm3\sigma$ contains 99.7% of the data, with the red circles at both tails holding the remaining roughly 0.1% + 0.1%. Then compute the **z-score**

$$
z=\left|\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right| ,
$$

and at the 99.7% confidence level treat $z>3$ as an anomaly.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.24 -->

![Slide 25 - Univariate data example](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-25.png)

The slide's example: mean temperatures {24.0, 28.9, 28.9, 29.0, 29.1, 29.1, 29.2, 29.2, 29.3, 29.4}, $\mu=28.61$ , and the slide gives $\sigma=\sqrt{2.29}=1.51$ , so 24 has $z=\frac{24-28.61}{1.51}\approx3.04>3$ and is judged an anomaly.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.25 -->

**Extension (checking the slide's numbers):** recomputing with the formula above, the sum of squared deviations is $23.849$ , the variance is $23.849/10\approx2.385$ (the slide writes 2.29), $\sigma\approx1.54$ and $z\approx2.99$ — just below 3. The method is unchanged, but the example shows that for points near a fixed threshold, the verdict is sensitive to the estimate of $\sigma$ .
<!-- Sources: L22 p.25 -->

### Multivariate: Mahalanobis distance

![Slide 26 - Multivariate normal and Mahalanobis distance](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-26.png)

Assume the data follows a multivariate normal distribution, described by a mean vector $\mu$ and a covariance matrix $\Sigma$ (the bubble at the top right gives the 2-D $\Sigma$ : the diagonal holds the two variances, the off-diagonal entries the covariance). The **Mahalanobis distance** measures how far a point is from the distribution's centre while taking into account how the features vary together:

$$
D_M(x)=\sqrt{(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)} .
$$

A large distance means the point is unlikely under this distribution, a potential outlier.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.26 -->

![Slide 28 - Euclidean vs Mahalanobis distance](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-28.png)

The data cloud is an ellipse tilted up to the right, and each point's shade shows its Mahalanobis distance. Look at the table below: by Euclidean distance B (7.1) is further from the centre than A (5.7); by Mahalanobis distance A (35) is more anomalous than B (24) — A departs from the direction in which the data slopes, while B lies roughly along the ellipse's long axis. When two features are strongly correlated, deviating along the correlation is not very unusual and deviating across it is, which is exactly why the Mahalanobis distance is used.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.28 -->

Then apply **Grubbs' test** to these Mahalanobis distances (treated as univariate and approximately normal) to find a single outlier:

$$
G=\frac{\max_i\lvert x_i-\mu\rvert}{\sigma}.
$$

The Mahalanobis distance is mainly useful for global anomalies and less effective for local, contextual or collective ones.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.27 -->

| Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- |
| Statistical tests are well understood and well validated | Data may be hard to model parametrically (multiple modes, variable density) |
| Gives a quantitative measure of how anomalous an object is | In high dimensions there may be too little data to estimate the true distribution |
<!-- Sources: L22 p.29 -->

## 5. Proximity, density and clustering methods

### Proximity-based

An object is an anomaly if its nearest neighbours are far away — if its "proximity" deviates significantly from that of most objects in the dataset. A common approach: outlier score = distance to the $k$ -th nearest neighbour (kNN distance is in [02 KNN](02-KNN.md)).
<!-- Sources: L22 p.31 -->

![Slide 32 - Proximity-based detection with 1 nearest neighbour](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-32.png)

![Slide 33 - Proximity-based detection with 5 nearest neighbours](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-33.png)

These two plots show the same data; darker means a higher score. With 1 nearest neighbour, the circled C at the top right is really two points close together and far from everything else; each is the other's nearest neighbour, so they are pale and missed. With 5 nearest neighbours the pair becomes clearly darker, and the scores of another circled group in the left cluster change as well. The score is very sensitive to $k$ .

![Slide 34 - Proximity-based detection near a dense cluster](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-34.png)

A different dataset, still with 5 nearest neighbours: C is black, the most suspicious; D is a single point right next to a very dense small cluster, and by absolute distance it is not far from its neighbours, so it is pale; meanwhile A on the edge of the sparse cluster on the left is darker. Is D an anomaly or not? Absolute distance alone cannot answer — this method copes poorly with data whose density varies widely.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.32-35 -->

Pros: a proximity measure is easier to define than a statistical distribution; it quantifies how anomalous an object is; it handles multiple modes naturally. Cons: $O(n^2)$ complexity; sensitive to $k$ ; works poorly when density varies widely.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.35 -->

### Density-based

![Slide 37 - Basic density-based detection](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-37.png)

Anomalies lie in regions of low density: the sparser the surroundings, the higher the outlier score. In the sketch at the top right, the lines from A to its three neighbours B, C and D are all long, so the density around A is low. Density can be the number of points within a fixed radius $d$ , or the inverse of the average distance to the $k$ nearest neighbours:

$$
\mathrm{density}(x,k)=\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{y\in N(x,k)}\mathrm{distance}(x,y)\right)^{-1}.
$$

Limitation: it uses **absolute** density, so it cannot adapt to local variation when the data's density is uneven.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.37 -->

![Slide 38 - Local density-based detection](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-38.png)

**Local density methods** compare a point's density with the average density of its $k$ nearest neighbours: in the sketch at the top right, A's neighbours are packed in a very dense clump, and A is much sparser than they are. Even if its region is not sparse globally, a point that is markedly sparser than its neighbours is an outlier. The slide's **LOF (local outlier factor)** score is the inverse of the relative density:

$$
\text{relative density}(x,k)=\left(\frac{\mathrm{density}(x,k)}{\frac{1}{k}\sum_{y\in N(x,k)}\mathrm{density}(y,k)}\right)^{-1}.
$$

Much greater than 1: far sparser than its neighbours, a likely anomaly; about 1 or less: similar to its neighbours. So in data with "one very dense cluster + one very sparse cluster", normal points in the sparse cluster get inflated outlier scores under absolute density, whereas under LOF they are as sparse as their neighbours and score about 1.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.38 -->

#### Example: LOF on one-dimensional data

![Slide 39 - LOF example setup](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-39.png)

The slide's example: the dataset is {1, 1.05, 1.1, 1.15, 1.2, 1.21, 1.3, 1.4, 1.45, 1.5, 4.55, 5.6, 6.8, 7.58, 8.6, 9.7, 10.3, 11.4, 12.3, 13.5}; using 2 nearest neighbours and Manhattan distance, compute the scores of $x=0.5$ and $x=4$ . The two arrows on the number line point at these query points (orange): 0.5 sits right next to the dense group on the left, and 4 lies in the sparse region to the right.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.39 -->

![Slide 41 - LOF for x = 0.5](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-41.png)

![Slide 43 - LOF for x = 4](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-43.png)

The two slides compute the neighbours' densities for each query point (the arcs at the top mark each neighbour's own 2 nearest neighbours) and the final scores:

| Query point | 2 neighbours | Own density | Neighbours' densities | Inverse relative density |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 1, 1.05 | $\big(\frac{0.5+0.55}{2}\big)^{-1}\approx1.9$ | 13.3, 20 | $\approx8.8$ (the slide writes 9.1) |
| 4 | 4.55, 5.6 | $\big(\frac{0.55+1.6}{2}\big)^{-1}\approx0.93$ | 0.61, 0.89 | $\approx0.81$ |
<!-- Sources: L22 p.40-44 -->

**Note:** recomputing the first row from the slide's own intermediate values gives $\frac{(13.3+20)/2}{1.9}\approx8.8$ , whereas slide p.41 writes 9.1; the conclusion is unaffected.

The key contrast: 0.5's absolute density (1.9) is actually higher than 4's (0.93), so absolute density alone would make 4 look more suspicious; but 0.5 sits right next to a very dense clump, and its relative density shows it is about nine times sparser than its neighbours, so it is an anomaly; 4 lies in a region that is sparse anyway, similar to its neighbours, so it is not.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.40-44 -->

Pros: quantifies how anomalous an object is; works even when the data's density varies. Cons: $O(n^2)$ ; parameters $k$ and the distance threshold $d$ must be chosen.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.45 -->

### Clustering-based

Anomalies are objects that do not strongly belong to any cluster. The issue: outliers may affect the initial formation of the clusters.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.47 -->

![Slide 48 - Cluster-based outlier factor](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-48.png)

The slide splits the procedure into four steps:

**Step 1, cluster:** apply a clustering algorithm (e.g. [K-means](11-unsupervised-learning.en.md)) with a similarity measure (e.g. Euclidean distance).

**Step 2, label clusters large or small:** sort the clusters by size and use the parameters $\alpha$ (a cumulative size threshold, e.g. 90% of points) and $\beta$ (a minimum size ratio, e.g. 5×) to separate the large clusters (LC) holding most of the data from the small clusters (SC) with few points.

**Step 3, compute CBOF for each point $t$ :**

$$
\mathrm{CBOF}(t)=\begin{cases}
\lvert C_i\rvert\cdot\mathrm{distance}(t,C_i) & \text{if } C_i\in LC\\
\lvert C_i\rvert\cdot\min_{C_j\in LC}\mathrm{distance}(t,C_j) & \text{if } C_i\in SC
\end{cases}
$$

**Step 4, flag:** the higher the score, the more likely an anomaly: points in small, isolated clusters, or points far from the centre of a large cluster, are flagged.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.48 -->

![Slide 49 - Cluster-based outlier scores](assets/15-anomaly-detection/slide-49.png)

In the figure the yellow and blue groups are the two large clusters and the green group at the top left is the small cluster; the red numbers are points' CBOF scores. Points on the edges of the large clusters, far from their centres, score higher, and the two isolated points at (5, 0) with 535.2 and at (10, 10) with 564.1 score highest.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.49 -->

A common two-phase approach: **point-level filtering** — iteratively remove points that significantly reduce clustering quality, treating them as individual anomalies; **cluster-level filtering** — discard small clusters far from the others, treating them as group anomalies.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.50 -->

Pros: some clustering techniques are $O(n)$ ; it extends the notion of an outlier from single objects to groups of objects. Cons: thresholds for large and small clusters are needed; sensitive to the number of clusters; outliers may affect the initial clusters.
<!-- Sources: L22 p.51 -->
